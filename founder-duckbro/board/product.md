# Product lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The pitch fits in one sentence, and it is funny.** "A serious fashion house, run by an unserious duck" tells a buyer what this is and who it is for. Most pre-launch brands can't do that.
- **The voice holds across the site.** "Be the first to quack", "If it doesn't quack, we'll fix it" and the joke behind the name all sound like the same brand. That kind of consistency is craft.
- **Some of the parts nobody sees have been specified.** The socks list a fibre blend (80/17/3), sizes S/M/L and a knitted (not printed) motif. The tote states 12oz canvas. The scarf states 40% lambswool.
- **The pre-order idea makes sense.** "We only make what's reserved" fits the limited-drop story and could keep stock risk low, if it works as described.
- **Buying looks simple to the customer.** €4 flat shipping, free over €50, 2–4 days in the EU and UK, Stripe checkout, 30-day returns.

## What would kill it (most dangerous first)
1. **The site contradicts itself in ways a customer will notice.** The banner says "Drop 003 is live" while every product says it has ended and is "Not available yet". "Almost Gone", "Restocked" and "Best Seller" badges sit on items nobody can buy. The homepage shows three 5-star quotes, but every product page says "No reviews yet". The "was" prices may never have been charged; nobody knows. A buyer who spots one of these will doubt the rest, including whether the €4.99 reservation will turn into a product. For a brand built on a joke, trust is the whole product.
2. **There is no focus.** Twelve SKUs across socks, bags, a cap, a scarf, a charm and keyrings, all at pre-order stage. If each product needs 100 reservations before production starts, spreading early demand across twelve items makes it less likely that any one item reaches the threshold. Bags, wool scarves and socks also need different suppliers and quality checks. The one thing is not clear yet.
3. **The first five minutes end in a wait with no date.** A customer who likes the joke pays €4.99 and gets a spot in a queue. The site gives no ship date, shows no progress toward 100, and does not say what happens if the threshold is never reached. The best moment of the experience, opening a parcel of duck socks, is the one the customer cannot see yet.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- If you could sell only one product, which would it be? (The Quack Pack is labelled Best Seller. Is that based on any sales?)
- How many reservations exist today, per product, and how many are paid?
- What happens to a €4.99 reservation if a product never reaches 100? Is it refunded, and when?
- Were the struck-through "was" prices ever charged? Where do the three homepage quotes come from?
- Who is the supplier, what are the minimum order quantities, and have you held a production sample of each item?
- What does a customer receive after reserving: an email, a progress count, a date?
- What is the unit cost and margin on the Quack Pack? (Unknown. This is an open question for the CFO.)

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Make every claim true.** Remove the badges on items that are not in stock, the "was" prices unless they were really charged, and any review that is not from a verified buyer. Fix the banner so it matches the products.
2. **Cut to one hero product**, probably a sock pack, for the next drop. Show a public reservation counter toward 100 and a dated promise of what happens either way.
3. **Design the whole reservation experience** before taking more money: the confirmation email, progress updates, a refund rule if the threshold is missed, and the unboxing.
4. **Show a production sample** that matches the published specs, with supplier, minimum order quantity and unit cost confirmed.
