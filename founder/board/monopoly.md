# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **Distribution already exists.** Seven live channels including Allegro, the main Polish marketplace, plus 17 Shopify storefronts. The 30-day Allegro sample shows 247 orders from about 8,330 views across six listings. Many small businesses never get this far.
- **Small B2B niches where TextilWorld could plausibly lead.** A medical scrubs maker that needs OEKO-TEX on every order, a Kraków fashion school, an army uniform supplier in the pipeline. Certified, repeat, spec-driven buying is the closest thing here to a market TextilWorld could own. The two active accounts bring in about 8,000 PLN/month together.
- **Some operational know-how.** There are package-size rules, courier dispute evidence and warehouse labels. This is not a moat yet, but it is the start of process knowledge that competitors may lack.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **It is a commodity sold in a crowded market.** The same imported fabric, sold by the meter, appears next to rival listings on the same Allegro search page. Where TextilWorld is priced below competitors (viscose, jersey) it sells well. Where it is priced above them (chiffon, velvet) it sells less. That points to buyers choosing on price. If so, profit gets competed away, and nothing stops a rival from matching the price in month six. We cannot tell how thin that profit is, because gross margin and landed cost are unknown.
2. **There is no durable advantage of any kind.** No proprietary product, no network effects, no scale advantage (the company is losing courier disputes rather than winning better terms) and no brand customers ask for by name. The CEO's long-term ideas (pet products, home electronics, hair care, fitness) would spread effort over more crowded markets instead of building depth in one.
3. **It runs at the mercy of platforms and suppliers.** Allegro, Amazon and Shopify set the rules for most of the business. Supply depends on China with a 50–70 day lead time, and 3 of 8 tracked SKUs are already at zero. A business that cannot keep its bestseller in stock cannot hold its position, let alone dominate.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What is gross margin per meter after landed cost, platform fees and shipping? (Unknown.)
- Who else sells OEKO-TEX-certified or medical-grade fabric to Polish scrubs makers, and how many such buyers are there? (Market size unknown.)
- What do you know about fabric buyers in Poland that your Allegro competitors do not? What is the secret?
- Do you have exclusive access to any product: a mill, a pattern, a certification route?
- What does it cost to win one B2B account, and how long do they stay? (CAC and repeat rate unknown.)
- Of all your revenue, what share comes from customers who would not switch for 1 PLN/m less?
- Why build a logistics center and new brands before owning one category?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Pick one small market and aim to dominate it,** for example certified fabric for Polish medical/workwear makers, or institutional supply to fashion schools. Define it, size it and name the competitors within 90 days.
2. **Build one real advantage there.** Options include exclusive or private-label fabric, certification paperwork as a standard service, guaranteed stock on core SKUs, and contract terms (the B2B portal with NET30) that make switching costly.
3. **Show the unit economics.** Present margin per meter and the cost to acquire a B2B account before any more spend on new channels (Emag, Cdiscount) or new categories.
4. **Drop the unrelated brand expansion** until the first niche is clearly won.
